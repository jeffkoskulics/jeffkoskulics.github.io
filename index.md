I'm a physicist who works on measurement — building the instruments that tell
you what is actually happening inside a process, and then making the answer
trustworthy enough to act on.

I'm a Senior Field Applications Engineer at [PDF Solutions](https://www.pdf.com/),
on the eProbe product line, where the work is semiconductor metrology and
process control: getting a measurement out of a wafer, understanding what the
signal really means, and turning it into something a fab can use to move yield.
It sits at the intersection I keep coming back to — optics and sensing on one
side, statistics and process behavior on the other.

Before that I spent nearly five years at [Blink Charging](https://blinkcharging.com/)
in EV charging and energy storage, as a Senior Product Development Engineer and
Product Development Manager. That work ran the length of the product: optics and
sensors, PCB and firmware, systems integration, failure analysis, and the
unglamorous stretch where a hardware design has to survive contact with pilot
manufacturing. I did my Ph.D. in physics at Stevens Institute of Technology.

What ties it together is a preference for root cause over workaround. Design of
experiments, statistical process control, careful failure analysis — these are
just the disciplined forms of refusing to accept a plausible story about a
system when you could instead go measure it. Most of the expensive mistakes I've
watched happen, mine included, came from acting on a theory that nobody had
bothered to test cheaply first.

I work in MATLAB, Python, and SolidWorks, and I'm a US citizen based in the
greater Phoenix area.

Outside of work I run an always-on home server on a fanless 2020 MacBook Air —
no package manager, no inbound ports, a NAS on a direct cable, and a growing
pile of scripts that each write a log you can read afterward. It is a small
system with real constraints, which makes it an honest place to practice the
same habits.

This site is where I write about that: measurement, instrumentation, hardware
that has to work, and the occasional debugging story with a moral.
